Classical statistics leans on strong assumptions, a known model family, correct specification, i.i.d. data, and this book starts by loosening them.

Push that far enough and you end up willing to treat a model as one black box machine, no promises about what's inside, no guarantee it's the "right" model for anything. The question this book actually starts from is what's left to say once you've given that up: how do you still get theoretically grounded statistics out of a system you're not allowed to look inside?

This first volume follows one answer to that question, **conformal prediction**, and the line of work that grew out of it: from prediction sets with a coverage guarantee, to *learn then test*, to controlling a general risk rather than just miscoverage.

> **Where this is going**
> First, *why conformal prediction* at all, and the general algorithm worked through on an example. Then *beyond CP*, where I lay out the limitations I see and where I think the research should go next. The rest of the book is paper reviews: [Learn then Test](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-applied-statistics/volume-19/issue-2/Learn-then-test--Calibrating-predictive-algorithms-to-achieve-risk/10.1214/24-AOAS1998.full), then [risk-controlling prediction sets](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.02703), and finally [conformal risk control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814) together with [conformal policy control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02196), looking at how these get applied in ML and decision making.
